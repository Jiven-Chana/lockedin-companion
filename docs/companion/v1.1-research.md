# Evidence for the v1.1 Progress redesign

**Status:** research note, 24 Nov 2026, written for [`v1.1-plan.md`](v1.1-plan.md). Jiven asked for a progression chart with a score, rewards and attention on gaps, and for the research behind it.

**How it was checked:** each source was looked up on 24 Nov 2026. The figures come from the paper's abstract or publisher page unless marked otherwise. Full texts were not read, so anything the deck relies on should be checked against the paper first. The `LIT-n` ids are the team's own (`research/findings-register.md` in the research repository); the new sources have no id until KAYZ adds them.

**One thing to say plainly:** the team's behaviour-change document (T20) says "gamification, streaks and points were considered and dropped", and rule R-6 says no streaks and no guilt. A visible score is a departure from that. The evidence below supports a specific, careful version of it and rules out others. It needs an entry in the team's `design-log.md`.

---

## 1. What the evidence supports

### Showing recorded progress helps people reach goals
- **Harkin et al. (2016),** *Psychological Bulletin* 142(2), 198–229, DOI 10.1037/bul0000025. Meta-analysis of 138 experiments (N = 19,951). Prompting people to monitor progress improved goal attainment (d+ = 0.40), and more so when progress was physically recorded or made public. *Figures from the accepted manuscript.*
- **In our own data:** C-5 (perceived cost runs ahead of real cost when nothing shows progress), and LIT-21 (Locke & Latham: goals work with feedback on progress).
- **So:** keep a real history and draw it. The app already logs every answer; nothing new has to be tracked.

### Compare with yourself and with a goal, not with other people
- **Jivet, Scheffel, Specht & Drachsler (2018),** *LAK '18*, 31–40, DOI 10.1145/3170358.3170421. Systematic review of learning dashboards for learners. Comparison with peers is the most common reference frame, and it is not perceived positively by all learners.
- **In our own data:** INT-02-a (social pressure put a student off), LIT-20, V-9 (no leaderboards).
- **So:** the chart compares you with your own past and with a steady pace to the end of term. No leaderboard.

### A dashboard has to lead to an action
- **Kaliisa et al. (2024),** *LAK '24*, DOI 10.1145/3636555.3636884. Systematic review of 38 studies. No evidence that learning analytics dashboards have delivered on improving achievement; most studies report negligible or small effects. Some show medium to large effects on engagement.
- **Jivet et al. (2017),** "Awareness is not enough" (a review of 26 dashboards): showing the data is not the same as supporting what to do next.
- **So:** every gap the page shows comes with one thing to do about it. This is Jiven's point that gaps should drive "revisit".

### Spreading study across subjects and coming back to them
- **Brunmair & Richter (2019),** *Psychological Bulletin* 145(11), 1029–1052, DOI 10.1037/bul0000209. Meta-analysis of 59 studies. Interleaving has a moderate overall benefit (Hedges' g = 0.42); smaller for mathematical tasks (g = 0.34), and not significant for expository texts.
- **Cepeda et al. (2006),** *Psychological Bulletin* 132(3), 354–380. 839 assessments in 317 experiments: distributed practice beats massed practice, and the best gap grows with how long the material must be remembered.
- **Already in the register:** LIT-17 (Dunlosky: practice testing and distributed practice rated highest), LIT-24.
- **Caveat:** interleaving is strongest for similar categories inside one subject. Across different modules the firmer support is spacing: time away and a return.
- **So:** show where the learning came from by module, and point at the module that has had none.

### Showing learners the model of what they know
- **Bull & Kay (2007, revised 2016),** the SMILI framework, *International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education*. Opening the system's model of the learner to the learner is used to promote reflection, planning and monitoring.
- **So:** the score is always explainable, down to the ideas behind it. Tapping a point says what happened.

## 2. What the evidence warns against

### Feedback that points at the person
- **Kluger & DeNisi (1996),** *Psychological Bulletin* 119, 254–284. 607 effect sizes, 23,663 observations. Feedback improves performance on average (d = 0.41), but over a third of feedback interventions made it worse. It works less well the more it moves attention from the task to the self.
- **So:** the page talks about ideas and modules ("GANs became solid", "nothing on HCI this week"), never about the student ("you are behind"). The gap to the steady-pace line is never printed as a number.

### Rewards given for doing the task
- **Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999),** *Psychological Bulletin* 125, 627–668. Meta-analysis of 128 studies. Rewards contingent on engaging, completing or performing undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = −0.40, −0.36, −0.28). *The same paper reports that verbal rewards, meaning positive feedback, enhanced it; that figure was not re-checked on 24 Nov.*
- **So:** rewards here are information about what the student did, earned and kept. Nothing is offered in advance as a prize for hitting a number.

### Leaderboards, and points as an end
- **Hanus & Fox (2015),** *Computers & Education* 80, 152–161. 71 students over a semester. The course with badges and a leaderboard ended with lower intrinsic motivation, satisfaction and final exam scores than the same course without them. *Findings from secondary summaries.*
- **Mekler et al. (2017),** *Computers in Human Behavior* 71, 525–534. Points, levels and leaderboards raised the quantity of work, and did not change intrinsic motivation or felt competence.
- **Sailer et al. (2017),** *Computers in Human Behavior* 69, 371–380. Badges, leaderboards and performance graphs raised competence need satisfaction and perceived meaningfulness. The task was order picking in a simulation, not study.
- **Already in the register:** LIT-19 (Sailer & Homner 2020: gamification has small positive effects overall).
- **So:** a performance graph and badges have some support; a leaderboard and levels do not earn their place. No levels (Jiven chose milestones only).

### Streaks
- **Already in the register:** LIT-23 (losses loom larger than gains), INT-01-i (a student abandoned Anki over 2,000 lapsed cards), R-6.
- **So:** no streak. No milestone depends on an unbroken chain; "three full weeks kept" counts any three weeks.

---

## 3. What this evidence does not settle

- **None of it tests this design.** The sources are about monitoring, dashboards, interleaving and game elements in general. Whether this score helps these students is a question for a user test.
- **The dip when ideas fade is a loss.** Prospect theory (LIT-23) says it will be felt more than the same gain. It is kept small, labelled "fading" and comes with one tap to get the idea back. A test should watch for it reading as punishment.
- **Points for starting a study block** are a reward for doing a task, the kind Deci et al. warn about. They are kept small beside the points for learning, and the discipline rewards are mostly milestones.
- **The steady-pace line can show someone below it.** That is feedback against a goal, which LIT-21 supports, and also a way to feel behind, which R-6 forbids. The wording and the missing number are the mitigation, not a proof.
