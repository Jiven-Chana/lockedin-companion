How should an explainer show an idea?

Today an explainer in Learn is a paragraph and one still diagram, some drawn by LockedIn and some by mermaid in its own style. You asked for one Blocks design, more dynamic, that works for any subject. Below are four ways to do it that differ in what the student does, not in how they look. All four are drawn by LockedIn from data the tutor supplies (V-33), so none uses mermaid's drawing. Every sample works: use it.

The student's question

"Show me how this works." Asked in the Learn stage, after a guess and before a check.

What LockedIn holds

The unit's ideas, the slide each came from, the tutor's words, and whatever the tutor is asked to supply as data.

What today's picture assumes

That seeing the whole diagram at once is enough, and that looking is learning.

How it could hurt

Too much at once (AFF-C). A wrong picture taught as true (INT-01-g). A long wait, or a large bill on a student's plan.

Try every design on One has numbers, one has none: the test of "widely applicable".

The GAN content is from your own Learn session of 28 Nov (slides 27, 29, 33; the misconception is the one your tutor checked). The HCI content was written for this page.

A

Build it up

The picture arrives one block at a time. The student sets the pace.

Learn

What the tutor supplies

The picture, plus for each step: which blocks are lit, a label or two, one sentence.

Evidence for

  • AFF-C (Strong): "Too much at once": show one step at a time.
  • INT-02-g: "even random squares and arrows on a page" help him; symbol-heavy pages make him switch off.
  • Look test, V01 on a text-heavy Learn step: "massive block of text looks ridiculous and I lose mental capability". On Blocks: "the blocks segment things a bit more".
  • Rey et al. 2019: across 56 investigations, segments the learner paces improved retention and transfer and lowered load. abstract read
  • Mayer & Chandler 2001: a learner-paced, segmented animation beat a continuous one on transfer (not on recall). from reviews
  • Schroeder & Cenkci 2018: words placed with the part they describe, g = 0.63 over 58 comparisons. abstract read

Evidence against, and how it could hurt

  • Q-6 (Strong): students rate doing above watching. Pressing Next is still watching.
  • LIT-42, expertise reversal: steps slow a student who already knows it. Needs "Show it all".
  • No student of ours has been shown a stepped picture. The support is by inference from AFF-C.
B

Change it and see

A small working model. The student moves something and the picture answers.

Learn

What the tutor supplies

The controls, the meters, and a formula for how each meter moves. LockedIn would need a safe way to run the tutor's formulas; in this sample they are written by hand. The engine's own demo maker has the AI write a whole web page instead: more to write (not measured here), and not in Blocks.

Evidence for

  • You, 28 Nov: "an interactive demo [that] fits the screen and lets users play around with it"; "I need it more dynamic".
  • Look test, V01, on why Blocks: "Last design felt more interactive."
  • Q-6: doing is rated above watching.
  • D'Angelo et al. 2014 (SRI review of STEM simulations): simulations with added support beat the same simulations without it, g+ = 0.49. figure from the report, via search
  • Tversky, Morrison & Bétrancourt 2002: where animation helped, interactivity was often the cause. from course summaries

Evidence against, and how it could hurt

  • Not widely applicable. Switch to the HCI idea: there is nothing to measure, so the amounts are invented.
  • INT-01-g (Strong): he does not trust an AI as the source of truth. A model the tutor wrote cannot be checked against a slide, and a wrong one teaches the wrong thing convincingly.
  • Lazonder & Harmsen 2016 (72 studies) and the SRI review: guided exploring teaches more than free play. It needs a prompt, as here. from summaries
  • The most to build. As data it costs about the same as the others (see the table); the engine's own demo maker, which has the AI write a whole page, would cost far more. That was not measured here.
C

Fill the gap

The picture has a hole. The student places a block in it, then sees why.

GuessBefore you are taught · nothing is marked

What the tutor supplies

The picture, plus for each gap: where it is, two or three blocks to choose from, and one line on each.

Evidence for

  • Q-6 (Strong): practice questions and explaining are the methods students rate best.
  • Q-7: 10 of 22 lean towards being questioned first.
  • Your v3.1 decision: a quick guess before an idea is taught. This is that guess, made on the picture.
  • LIT-26, Pan & Carpenter 2023: guessing before being taught helps, when the right answer is studied straight after.
  • LIT-16, LIT-17: retrieval beats rereading on a delayed test.

Evidence against, and how it could hurt

  • Look test: two of three found Blocks' wrong-answer screen the most pressuring; V04 said it would "piss him off" by the third. So: lilac, small, never a score.
  • Q-7: 8 of 22 want to be shown first. It must be skippable.
  • LIT-38, Stull & Mayer 2007: students who built an organiser did no better than those who were given it, and took longer.
  • It explains only after the guess: by itself it is a question, not an explainer.
D

Two cases, compared

The same picture twice, differing in what matters. The student finds the difference.

Learn

What the tutor supplies

The picture, two versions of its labels, and one line on each difference.

Evidence for

  • Your own session: the tutor's check was built on a wrong script ("changes the parameters of both D and G"). This puts that mistake beside the right version.
  • You, 28 Nov: walk through a worked solution, then "attempt an augmentation of the question myself": an original and its variation are two cases.
  • Alfieri, Nokes-Malach & Schunn 2013: comparing cases beat other instruction across 57 experiments (about d = 0.50), more so when the principle is stated after the comparison. figure from a summary: check before quoting

Evidence against, and how it could hurt

  • AFF-C; V04 on Blocks' fuller screens: "information overload". Two pictures is twice as much at once.
  • Not every idea has a telling contrast. Where it has none, the second case is padding.
  • A wrong case that is not clearly marked can be remembered as the right one.
  • None of our students asked for comparisons. The primary evidence is your session alone.

"AI usage" is the size of the data for the two samples (GAN, then the gulfs), counted as characters divided by four, on top of the picture itself (about 120 and 90). What the evidence picks

A as the explainer, with C as its first step

A is the only design supported by a Strong finding of ours, by two meta-analyses, and it works on both sample ideas. Its known weakness is that it is watching, and C answers exactly that with our next-strongest finding (Q-6), and already matches the Guess stage you approved. So: the picture opens with one gap to guess (skippable), then builds up step by step, with "Show it all" for a student who knows it.

B is the one you asked for by name, and the evidence does not carry it as the default: it fails the "any subject" test, is the most to build, and is the riskiest when the tutor is wrong. It could be offered later, only on ideas with numbers, and only when the student asks. D fits one moment well: after a missed check, to put the student's mistake beside the right version.

What is not known: no student has seen any of these four. Monday's hi-fi test is the first chance to watch one being used.
